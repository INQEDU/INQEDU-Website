From spotting techniques to analysing them

Naming a metaphor earns very little. This is the three-step habit that turns an observation into analysis.

Teachers write "analyse, don't describe" in the margin more than any other comment. It is hard to act on because description and analysis can look similar. The difference is what the sentence does after it names the feature.

What description looks like

Blake uses repetition in the word "charter'd" to emphasise it.

This is true and it says almost nothing. Every repetition emphasises. The sentence could be written about any repeated word in any text.

The three steps

  1. Choice. What exactly did the writer choose? Quote the smallest piece that carries the effect.
  2. Effect. What does that choice make a reader notice, picture or feel? Be specific to these words.
  3. Meaning. How does that effect serve the larger idea of the text, and your argument about it?

What analysis looks like

Blake applies "charter'd" first to a street and then to the Thames. A street can be owned, but a river is a natural thing, so the second use is unsettling. The repetition suggests that in this London even nature has been mapped and claimed, which prepares us for a poem where people, too, are marked and held.

The feature is the same. The second version explains why this word matters in this poem, and ties it to an argument.

A quick self-check

After every sentence that names a technique, ask "so what?" If your next sentence answers that question, you are analysing. If it moves on to another technique, go back.
